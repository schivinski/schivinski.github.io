@@small@@ Commentary on the Many Analysts Religion Project (Hoogeveen et al., Religion, Brain & Behavior, 13(3)).

@@p@@ The Many Analysts Religion Project (MARP) follows in a tradition of many analysts projects that have examined how variations in analytic choices can affect results (Silberzahn & Uhlmann, 2015). One of the core strengths of the many analysts approach is that there are no direct incentives for analysis teams to attempt to achieve statistical significance (Silberzahn et al., 2018). However, in this commentary we suggest that a lack of direct incentives to achieve particular outcomes can be a double-edged sword. We use our own submissions to the MARP project to illustrate our point.^{1}

@@p@@ In our submissions we went to some trouble to develop multilevel models that were suitable for testing the focal hypotheses of the MARP project. Nonetheless, our submissions shared the same potentially consequential limitation: we ignored issues pertaining to construct validity.^{2} In particular, consider how each team developed a religiosity predictor variable. Both of our teams operationalized this variable on the basis of intuitive judgements: Team 039 operationalised religiosity by sum scoring four out of the nine religiosity items, picking the two “best” behaviour-focused items and the two “best” belief-focused items in order to achieve a sense of “balance” between behaviour and belief in the variable; while Team 175 operationalized religiosity by sum scoring all nine items, feeling that a comprehensive coverage of all available religiosity items was most appropriate. The fact that we made different decisions for operationalising this variable is not of serious concern (after all, a focus of many analyst projects is to examine the extent to which different analytic decisions influence results). However, what is of concern is that neither team assessed the construct validity of the religiosity predictor variable. This is potentially problematic because using predictor variables with poor construct validity can invalidate analyses (Slaney, 2017). This lacuna in our submissions could be interpreted as manifesting a “measurement-schmesurement” attitude that is prevalent in the social sciences, whereby important measurement issues are down-played or entirely ignored (Flake & Fried, 2020; Flake, Pek, & Hehman, 2017; Hussey & Hughes, 2020). In conducting background reading for this commentary (i.e., after submitting our pre-registrations and analyses) we learnt that (1) unjustified sum scoring can have adverse consequences (McNeish & Wolf, 2020) and that (2) a failure to achieve measurement invariance for psychometric instruments across cultures can throw the validity of analyses into doubt (Boer, Hanke, & He, 2018; Fischer & Karl, 2019; van de Vijver & Leung, 2021).

@@p@@ Why did we fail to attend to construct validity in our submissions? We suggest that the incentive structure of the MARP project (which is typical of analysts projects) may have contributed.^{3} While developing our respective pre-registrations we could not help but be aware that our submission would be merely one among many and, thus, would have little discernible impact on the overall success of the project. Given that our teams have expertise in multilevel modelling, it was not overly time-consuming (and was rather enjoyable!) for us to put careful consideration in the pre-registrations of our multilevel models. Thus, it is not surprising that this aspect of our analyses is strong (or so we hope!). By contrast, our teams have little experience with construct validation in the context of cross-cultural research, meaning that it would have necessitated a considerable investment of time and effort to study the relevant literature in order to develop pre-registrations that thoroughly addressed key issues. Consequently, it is not surprising that construct validation failed to feature in our submissions. Had there been direct incentives for us to carefully attend to construct validation we may well have done so.

@@p@@ We strongly suspect that our MARP project submissions were not the only ones with limitations that could plausibly be attributed to a lack of direct incentives. While submissions will have a variety of strengths and weaknesses, we anticipate that insufficient attention being paid to construct validation will be a recurring limitation (given the ubiquity of the aforementioned “measurement-schmesurement” attitude in the social sciences). In fact, we would like to make a prediction: very few analysis teams have made any attempt to assess the construct validity of the variables they used in their analyses.^{4} If our prediction proves to be correct then we would hasten to emphasise that this should not be taken as support for the traditional single team approach because it has a poor track-record in terms of assessing the construct validity of measures. Rather, such a finding would provide evidence that the status quo of poor measurement practices in the social sciences is not ipso facto resolved by adopting a many analysts approach. Moreover, such a finding would suggest that an important direction for future work is to explore whether analysis teams can be incentivised to carefully consider all key dimensions of their submissions. This might involve developing procedures by which the strongest submissions are identified and the authors of these submissions are rewarded (by placing their names in a more prominent position in the author list; by inviting them to write a commentary about their submission; by giving them awards or cash prizes; etc). Alternatively (or in addition), this might involve incentivising researchers to form analysis teams that bring together diverse analytic skills and domain specific knowledge.

@@h2@@ Notes

@@p@@ ^{1} This commentary was written by members of two analysis teams: Ross and Sulik (along with Tappin) are Team 039, and Buczny and Schivinski are Team 175.

@@p@@ ^{2} The term “construct validity” encompasses a variety of subcomponents that have been classified in different ways (Slaney, 2017), and not all subcomponents could be assessed using the materials available to us in the MARP project. However, at least three important subcomponents (some of which are classified as “reliability” rather than “validity” according to some measurement classifications) could have been examined using available data: internal consistency, factor structure, and measurement invariance across cultures.

@@p@@ ^{3} We say “may have” contributed because we are not confident that we have good introspective access to what motivated our analysis decisions.

@@p@@ ^{4} We invite the leaders of the MARP project to test our prediction by coding each submission according to whether or not there is evidence of an attempt to assess construct validity.
